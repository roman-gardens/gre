## Garden Description
<!-- This is the main text describing the garden -->
The villa is about 2.75 km west of the center of the town of Saint-Sever <!--[Saint-Sever](link) -->. When the villa was built in the 4th century, the bank of Adour was probably much closer than it is today and, in fact, ran where there is now a sharp drop of several meters in the land running along the 35 meter contour line. As shown in the survey below, this bank would have been just thirty or forty meters in front of the as yet unexposed northern part of the villa, which is presumed to have been the proprietor's residence. South of the modern road lie the excavations, thought to be the entertaining and  service area, while further south would have been the quarters of the servants. The red shading in this figure indicates areas where there is evidence of the Gallo-Roman villa, including the *pars rustica* <!--[*pars rustica*](link) -->,  have been found. Following the collapse of Roman power, there were many Christian burials in the area now excavated, especially in and around the east courtyard. The west corner of the south gallery of this courtyard was free of burials and shows other signs of having become the paleochristian cemetery chapel, the church of Saint Peter of the Ruins, or, in Gascon, *Gleyzia Saint-Pé-de-Mazères*. Without having ever become an imposing structure, the *Gleyzia* came into possession of considerable land, as indicated by the circled parcel numbers in the survey. In the 14th century, the land had become a *caverie*<!--[*caverie*](link) -->, the estate of a minor nobleman who, at least etymologically, owed his lord military service on horseback. After the Revolution, it became several *métairies*<!--[*métairies*](link) -->, or properties held by sharecroppers, and thence eventually the cornfield of Jean Lafitte, who generously allowed archaeological exploration. By 2005, it was the property of Paul Dubedat, M.D., who had led the excavations since 1969.

The Gallo-Roman builder obviously preferred the riverfront property with its perfectly flat, rich bottom land to the gently sloping hill with a broad vista just to the south. His choice seems to have caused him considerable problems with water-soaked soil, for he built an extensive system of drains under the rooms of the house.

This vast villa of at least 2700 square meters was situated on an alluvial terrace<!--[*alluvial terrace*](link) --> (36 m above sea level) on the left bank of the Adour. It was noted and explored in the 19th century; modern excavation began in 1969 and has continued up to the present under the direction of Dr. Paul Dubedat. Occupation of the site may go back to the early empire, but the main phase of construction was in the fourth or fifth centuries. 

The villa is a rare case in domestic architecture of two very large, twin, symmetrically placed peristyles<!--[*peristyles*](link) --> of almost the same size. The floors of the galleries surrounding all four sides of both peristyles were completely covered with mosaics<!--[*mosaics*](link) -->. The eastern peristyle (31 x 25.5 m) was slightly larger (820 compared to 800 m2) and had geometric and plant motifs<!--[*motifs*](link) --> such as vines and stalks of acanthus leaves in the mosaics. The western peristyle gave access to baths.

The peristyles look like ideal places for gardens. They were not paved, and the soil throughout the area is rich bottom land. In his book, Dubedat says nothing about the courtyards in Gallo-Roman times; in conversation, he said he had no direct evidence on the question of whether or not they were planted.
